What Does This Company Actually Do?

The Simple Story

Training and running today's AI models needs huge numbers of specialized computer chips called GPUs. Most companies don't want to buy and manage thousands of these chips themselves, so CoreWeave buys them, wires them together into giant data centers, and rents out the computing power by the hour — like renting a fleet of cranes instead of buying your own.

Who Actually Buys It

Its customers are the biggest names in AI: Microsoft, OpenAI, and Meta are its largest, using CoreWeave's capacity to train and run their AI models when they can't build enough of their own data centers fast enough. Contracts run for multiple years and are worth billions of dollars each.

Business Model

How They Make Money

  • Multi-year contracts renting out GPU computing capacity, billed by usage or reserved capacity
  • Long-term deals with hyperscalers and AI labs (Microsoft, OpenAI, Meta) that lock in revenue for years
  • New bookings from data-center expansion as power capacity comes online

Why the Model Is Clever

  • Contracted backlog ($99.4B) is revenue that's already sold, just waiting to be delivered — far more predictable than typical tech-company revenue
  • Deep, early relationship with Nvidia gives it faster access to the newest chips than many competitors
  • Purpose-built for AI workloads specifically, letting it move faster than general-purpose cloud providers retrofitting for AI
Hidden Catalyst Deep Dive

The Opportunity

On July 1, 2026, Meta announced "Meta Compute" — a service that rents out its own GPU capacity by the hour, the same business CoreWeave is in. The stock fell roughly 35% on the news and another 11.4% weeks later as the market priced in Meta becoming a direct competitor. But the detail getting lost: Meta is contractually locked into a $35 billion CoreWeave deal running through 2032, and most analysts covering the story argue Meta cannot legally resell that leased capacity to third parties. If that reading holds, the "competitor" narrative is more of a long-term strategic worry than a near-term hit to CoreWeave's contracted revenue.

Why This Company Wins

CoreWeave built its GPU fleet, power capacity, and Nvidia relationship years before hyperscalers turned their own infrastructure into a rentable product. A $99.4 billion backlog — nearly 16 times its trailing annual revenue — is already signed and largely insulated from short-term competitive noise, buying years of runway to prove out the model.

Bottom Line Verdict [EDITORIAL OPINION]

CoreWeave is like a construction company that just signed sixteen years of contracted work, then watched its stock crash because one client mentioned it might someday build its own equipment too.

The long-term case rests on real, contracted demand: a $99.4 billion backlog, a head start in power capacity and Nvidia access, and continued AI infrastructure spending that no hyperscaler can fully build in-house fast enough. If that backlog converts to revenue roughly as scheduled, today's price looks cheap relative to where analysts see the stock in a year.

The near-term risk is just as real — this is a debt-funded, loss-making, customer-concentrated business whose largest clients are also becoming its competitors, and the Meta selloff shows how fast sentiment can turn. This is a name for investors with a high risk tolerance and at least a 2-3 year horizon, not a short-term trade on the current volatility.
