What Does This Company Actually Do?

The Simple Story

Imagine the military needs a thousand drones instead of ten very expensive fighter jets — Kratos is the company that builds the cheaper, faster-to-produce option. It makes target drones, combat drones, hypersonic test vehicles (things that fly faster than five times the speed of sound), and satellite ground systems, all designed to be built quickly and affordably rather than as one-off masterpieces.

Who Actually Buys It

Almost all of its revenue comes from the U.S. government — the Department of Defense, the Missile Defense Agency, and branches like the Marine Corps and Air Force — plus a smaller amount from allied foreign militaries. Contracts are won through competitive bidding and can run for years once a program is selected.

Business Model

How They Make Money

  • Multi-year government contracts for drones, target systems, and space/satellite hardware
  • Hypersonic test vehicle and testing-infrastructure contracts tied to national defense modernization priorities
  • C5ISR (command, control, communications) electronics sold into both defense and adjacent government programs

Why the Model Is Clever

  • "Affordable mass" positioning matches a real Pentagon strategic shift toward many cheap, attritable systems instead of few expensive ones
  • Record backlog of $2.01B and a 1.6x book-to-bill ratio give unusually strong revenue visibility for a growth-stage defense contractor
  • Owning manufacturing capacity for rapid, low-cost drone production is hard for legacy primes to replicate quickly
Hidden Catalyst Deep Dive
"Kratos has not included expected revenue from large-scale Valkyrie drone production in its base forecast for fiscal 2026 or 2027" — meaning the company's own guidance is arguably understating its own upside.

The Opportunity

The XQ-58 Valkyrie is an autonomous combat drone Kratos builds in partnership with Northrop Grumman for the U.S. Marine Corps. It's currently moving through mission evaluation, with a path toward low-rate initial production and, eventually, full-rate production. Critically, management has stated this program's large-scale production revenue is not baked into current 2026 or 2027 guidance — the numbers already being raised twice this year don't even assume this catalyst lands.

Why This Company Wins

Kratos's whole engineering culture is built around designing for low-cost, rapid manufacturing rather than maximum performance at any price — exactly what the Pentagon's "affordable mass" doctrine calls for. Teaming directly with Northrop Grumman, one of the largest defense primes, gives Valkyrie a credible integration and sales channel that a smaller drone-only competitor couldn't match.

Bottom Line Verdict [EDITORIAL OPINION]
The Verdict

Kratos looks like a shipyard building the Pentagon's next generation of cheap, disposable ships while everyone else still argues over battleships — the strategic wind is at its back, but shipyards are expensive to run before the fleet ships out.

The long-term case is believable: record backlog, guidance raised twice, direct alignment with the Pentagon's shift toward affordable, mass-produced systems, and a Valkyrie drone catalyst that management hasn't even included in its own numbers yet. If hypersonics scales toward its 2028 target and Valkyrie moves to production, today's valuation could look reasonable in hindsight.

The near-term pain is real: a 273x P/E and negative free cash flow leave almost no cushion, and the entire bull case depends on programs landing roughly on the timeline management expects. Investors comfortable with defense-program timing risk and dilution risk can reasonably hold on a 2-3 year horizon; more conservative investors may want to wait for confirmation from an actual Valkyrie production decision before adding.
