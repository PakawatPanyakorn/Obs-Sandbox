What Does This Company Actually Do?

The Simple Story

Micron makes memory chips — the parts of a computer that remember things while it's working, like a very fast notepad next to a chef's cutting board. If the processor is the chef, memory chips are the counter space the chef needs to work quickly. The more complicated the meal (or the AI model), the more counter space is needed — and that's exactly what's driving unprecedented demand for Micron's chips right now.

Who Actually Buys It

Micron sells almost entirely to other companies, not consumers directly: cloud giants building AI data centers (now its single largest and fastest-growing customer category), phone and PC makers who put memory in every device they build, and carmakers whose vehicles carry increasing amounts of onboard computing.

Business Model

How They Make Money

  • Selling DRAM chips (76% of revenue) — fast, short-term memory used in servers, PCs, and phones
  • Selling NAND flash chips (24% of revenue) — longer-term storage used in SSDs and phones
  • High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM) — a premium DRAM variant built specifically for AI chips, commanding much higher prices than standard memory

Why the Model Is Clever

  • Building competitive memory fabs costs many billions of dollars and takes years, so only three companies worldwide can compete at scale — that scarcity is Micron's real structural edge
  • Locking in multi-year "take-or-pay" customer contracts converts a historically boom-bust commodity business into something closer to a subscription — see Hidden Catalyst Deep Dive below
Hidden Catalyst Deep Dive
Roughly half of Micron's future revenue is already under contract — locked in years ahead of time, through Strategic Customer Agreements backed by $22B in customer cash deposits.

The Opportunity

Most investors are still pricing Micron as a normal cyclical memory company — great this quarter, doomed to crash next cycle. What's underreported is that Micron has converted a meaningful share of the current AI memory boom into Strategic Customer Agreements: multi-year, take-or-pay supply contracts (reportedly including a partnership tied to Anthropic's AI compute buildout) that lock in roughly $100B in aggregate future revenue, backed by around $22B in customer deposits already collected. That's a structural change in how memory demand gets monetized, not just a strong quarter.

Why This Company Wins

The DRAM market has consolidated into a genuine three-player oligopoly — Micron, Samsung, and SK Hynix — after years of unprofitable overcapacity drove out weaker competitors. Of the three, Micron has been the most aggressive about converting AI-era demand into binding contracts with cash already in hand, which reduces its own downside if AI capital spending ever slows, unlike prior cycles where Micron simply sold into the spot market and rode the crash down with everyone else.

Bottom Line Verdict [EDITORIAL OPINION]
The Verdict

Micron today is like a farmer during the best harvest in a generation — the barns are overflowing with cash, but everyone remembers the droughts, and nobody knows exactly when the weather turns.

The long-term story is believable: the DRAM market has consolidated into a genuine three-player oligopoly, and Micron has converted this AI-driven memory boom into roughly $100B of contracted, deposit-backed revenue — a structural change from prior cycles where Micron simply rode the spot market up and back down.

But the near-term pain is real: China's CXMT is closing the technology gap faster than expected, a synchronized industry-wide capex race raises genuine oversupply risk by 2027-2028, and export-control policy could cut either for or against Micron with little warning. Long-term investors with a 2-3+ year horizon are best positioned to ride out this volatility; short-term traders should expect continued sharp swings around every China and export-policy headline.
